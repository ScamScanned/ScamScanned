ScamScanned Recovery Guide

Get your money back

The order never shipped. Or it arrived unlike the listing, or the seller went quiet mid-refund. This is the recovery ladder in exact order — free, no email gate, no course, no upsell.

Rule zero: the clock matters more than the argument. Most lost money isn't lost to the seller — it's lost past a deadline nobody read. Your order page shows when the platform window closes and how the claim opens. Find it first, put it in your calendar today, then work the ladder below in order.

Step one, every time: the record

  1. Screenshot the listing with the web address visible, plus the order confirmation email and every message you exchange with the seller. Listings get deleted; emails don't. Ten screenshots now is the evidence you'll need later.
  2. Write a 5-line chronology while it's fresh: order date, payment amount, the promised-by date you were shown, what actually happened, dates. Facts only — no adjectives. Claims read by reviewers and disputes teams open better with chronology than with emotion, and facts get funded faster.

The Amazon ladder (third-party seller orders)

1. Refund request in Your Orders — before anything else

Open the order, choose Problem with order, and send a refund request to the seller first, whatever you're feeling. The marketplace guarantees expect you to give the seller one documented attempt, and a skipped step can sink the next one. Note the date you sent it — that date is now part of your chronology.

2. File the A-to-z claim

If the seller doesn't respond or the resolution isn't right, return to the order and choose the claim option. A-to-z covers third-party orders that never arrived, arrived damaged or materially unlike the listing, or where the refund request went unanswered. Paste the chronology from step one. Amazon decides against the published terms of the guarantee, and approved refunds return to your original payment method.

The one-way-door rule: filing a card dispute generally closes the A-to-z route on that same order. Take the A-to-z lane first for marketplace orders; move to your card only after the platform lane is closed or stalled.

3. If the item arrives mid-dispute

Don't restart anything and don't place new orders with that seller. Keep the package unopened where possible, note delivery against the promised window, and let the open claim decide the outcome — the claim, not the delivery, is what closes this.

The card lane (second, not first)

Your card issuer can reverse a payment, but issuers expect the merchant to get one attempt first, and dispute clocks run separately from marketplace windows. If the platform path is exhausted or past its window: collect your evidence pack (screenshots, chronology, the platform's own case outcome), then call the number on the card and ask to open a dispute. Keep the case number. If the dispute is approved, original payments come back through the issuer's process.

Other marketplaces, quickly

Walmart

Marketplace items carry delivery protections — start from the order in your account (help: help.walmart.com).

eBay

eBay Money Back Guarantee: open the case from your order, then escalate if it stalls (help: ebay.com/help).

Etsy

Order issues run through Purchases & Reviews → buyer protection (help: help.etsy.com).

PayPal purchases

Open the dispute in the Resolution Center inside the published window (180 days for most purchase problems); it escalates to a claim if unresolved.

Facebook Marketplace

Report from the listing; if you paid off-platform, bank routes are weak — next time use the platform's own checkout so the guarantee applies.

Craigslist (& cash trade)

Little recourse exists by design: no prepay, no money transfers, deals in person. See the warning box below.

Report it — this is the part that protects the next person

  • The marketplace: use the report controls on the listing or order. Aggregate reports are what trip platform-level enforcement on a seller.
  • The FTC (US): reportfraud.ftc.gov — three minutes, and each report feeds the national complaint record that regulators actually act on.
  • Money actually lost? Also file with the FBI's IC3 (ic3.gov).
  • Open the trail, not a fight: public reporting is the durable part. Never publish your screenshots along with personal details.

Beware the second wave. After a bad purchase, random messages often arrive claiming “recovery agents” who can retrieve your funds for a fee, or “support” calling from a number you didn't contact, needing card details to “verify” things. Nobody legitimate cold-contacts you to return your money. Real recovery is the ladder above, direct from you, on the platform's own pages.

A field pattern worth learning

An early user reported this sequence: a popular-product listing with a healthy delivery promise, polished product photos that looked machine-generated, no reviews yet, and an order that never showed any delivery movement — the item was likely never in stock. The teachable part isn't the photos; it's the absence of history.
